Leather and the Legacy of Luxury

For decades, luxury fashion has been built on the prestige of animal leather. From buttery calfskin to exotic hides, heritage houses have tied their reputations to materials that symbolize rarity and status. Owning a leather handbag has long been marketed as the ultimate marker of sophistication.

In fact, the rarer the animal, the higher the value. Exotic skins like crocodile, ostrich, and python have been auctioned for staggering sums, not only for craftsmanship but because of their perceived scarcity. Scarcity fuels prestige—and this has kept animal leather firmly positioned at the center of luxury.

But as the world shifts toward conscious living, one question keeps coming up: why do most luxury brands still avoid vegan handbags?

Tradition Over Innovation

Many luxury houses are steeped in tradition. Their archives are leather-bound—quite literally. Their ateliers have perfected techniques passed down for centuries, rooted in animal hides.

To these brands, leather is luxury. Replacing it with cactus fiber or mushroom mycelium feels like rewriting their entire identity. After generations of conditioning consumers to equate “leather” with “premium,” it’s difficult to unlearn and see luxury in another material. For heritage houses, abandoning this legacy feels like abandoning their DNA.


Craftsmanship and Familiarity

Across Europe, entire towns are devoted to leather craftsmanship, where tanning and finishing are integral to local economies. Skilled artisans spend lifetimes mastering the feel, weight, and stitching techniques of animal hides.

Vegan materials—whether apple skin, cactus, or mushroom-based—behave differently under the knife and needle. They require new methods of handling, cutting, and finishing. For traditional workshops, that means retraining artisans and retooling facilities—a daunting prospect for brands that have operated the same way for centuries.

The Quality Question

Luxury prides itself on longevity. A calfskin bag softens, patinas, and lasts for decades, often becoming an heirloom piece. Early vegan leathers, however, didn’t always offer the same durability. Many were synthetic-based materials prone to peeling, which created the misconception that “vegan” equaled “cheap” or “fast fashion.”

Though modern innovations in plant-based leathers are far more advanced, luxury houses remain cautious. Their reputations are built on timelessness, and they are reluctant to risk customer trust until materials have decades of proven resilience behind them.


Supply Chain Realities

The animal leather industry has a deeply entrenched supply chain. Leather is produced globally, standardized, and readily available at scale. By contrast, vegan leathers are often developed by smaller startups in limited batches. Mushroom and cactus leathers, for example, are still in early production stages.

For luxury houses operating at global scale, limited supply can feel impractical—even if the material itself is beautiful and sustainable. Until these new alternatives reach industrial availability, many big brands will hesitate.


Sustainability—or the Story of It

Luxury often frames leather as “sustainable,” claiming hides are simply by-products of the meat industry. The reality, however, is more complex. Leather sales are a multi-billion-dollar market and often a significant profit driver for meat and dairy industries. For many farmers, hides are not waste—they are a major source of revenue. This demand perpetuates the cycle of breeding and slaughter.

Meanwhile, some luxury brands have leaned on “eco-tanned” collections—marketing animal leather as more sustainable without addressing the core issue of animal cruelty or industrial resource use. Fully vegan alternatives are often sidelined in favor of narratives that preserve the leather legacy.


A Shift Is Underway

Despite these hurdles, the shift has begun. Consumer demand—especially from Millennials and Gen Z—is increasingly clear: cruelty-free, conscious design is the future of fashion. In recent years, mycelium (mushroom leather) and cactus leather have gained global recognition for their beauty, durability, and innovation. Luxury is beginning to explore these alternatives, though cautiously.
